Definition — quote this

“A genetic algorithm is a variant of stochastic beam search in which successor states are generated by combining two parent states rather than by modifying a single state.”

8-Queens · Part 1

How a chessboard becomes a string of digits

A chromosome for 8-queens is eight digits. The position in the string is the column; the digit is the row that column's queen sits in.

Reading the chromosome 24748552

column   1  2  3  4  5  6  7  8
digit     2  4  7  4  8  5  5  2
↑ column 3's queen is in row 7

This encoding is clever for one reason worth stating in an answer: because each column gets exactly one digit, two queens can never share a column. The representation removes one whole class of conflict for free, so you only ever check rows and diagonals.

8-Queens · Part 2

Counting attacks without losing your place

Two queens attack each other if they share a row, or if they share a diagonal. The diagonal test is pure arithmetic — no need to trace lines with your finger:

The diagonal test

two queens are on a diagonal ⟺ |Δcolumn| = |Δrow|

Column 3 (row 7) and column 8 (row 2): Δcolumn = 5, Δrow = 5. Equal, so they attack.

The method that stops double-counting

Work left to right. For each queen, only look rightward at the queens still ahead of it. Every pair then gets counted exactly once, and your running total can't drift.

Here is 24748552 broken into its four attacking pairs — each one on its own board, with the line of attack shaded.

PairRowsΔcolΔrowReason
col 1 & col 82, 270Same row
col 2 & col 44, 420Same row
col 3 & col 87, 255Diagonal — Δ equal
col 6 & col 75, 510Same row
Attacking pairs4

Your 8Queen slides tally the same thing column by column — 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, which sums to 4. Same answer, counted from the other direction.

Now convert attacks into fitness

max pairs for 8 queens  =  C(8,2)  =  (8 × 7) ÷ 2  =  28
fitness(24748552)  =  28 − 4  =  24 non-attacking pairs

general form  =  C(n,2)  =  n(n−1) ÷ 2  =  n! ÷ [2!(n−2)!]

Fitness counts the pairs that are safe, not the ones that clash. Higher is better, a perfect 8-queens solution scores 28, and if you write “4” when the question asks for fitness you lose the mark.

    Operator 1

    Selection — spinning the roulette wheel

    Selection decides which chromosomes become parents. The rule is that fitter chromosomes should reproduce more often, but weaker ones must keep a real chance, otherwise the population loses diversity and the search converges on a local optimum.

    Roulette wheel selection gives every chromosome a slice of a wheel proportional to its fitness, then spins once per parent needed.

    1. Evaluate

      Compute f(x) for every chromosome in the population.

    2. Total

      Add them up: F = Σfi. This is the size of the whole wheel.

    3. Probability

      Each chromosome's share is pi = fi / F. These must sum to 1.00 — a fast way to check your arithmetic.

    4. Cumulative bins

      Stack the probabilities into ranges. Chromosome 1 owns 0 → p₁, chromosome 2 owns p₁ → p₁+p₂, and so on up to 1.00.

    5. Spin

      Generate a random number r in [0, 1]. Whichever bin contains r names the parent. Repeat until you have enough parents.

    Here is the wheel from your own worked example. Notice how string 3 owns more than a third of it, and string 5 owns nothing at all.

    The other selection schemes

    If the question says “compare” or “name two other methods”, these are the ones to reach for.

    SchemeHow it worksWhy you'd use it
    Roulette wheelSlice of the wheel ∝ fitnessThe standard, simple to compute
    RankSort the population, slice ∝ rank instead of raw fitnessStops one enormous fitness dominating the wheel; also helps when all fitnesses are close together
    TournamentPick k chromosomes at random, the fittest winsCheap, and selection pressure is tunable via k
    ElitismCopy the best 1–2 chromosomes unchanged into the next generationGuarantees the best solution found is never destroyed by crossover or mutation
    Steady-stateReplace only a few of the worst each generationSlow, stable convergence

    The negative-fitness rule in your course

    When f(x) comes out negative, this course clamps it to 0. A chromosome with fitness 0 gets probability 0 and therefore no bin on the wheel at all — it can never be chosen as a parent. String 5 in the worked example below is exactly this case: x = 5 gives f(x) = −2.5, recorded as 0.

    Heuristic

    The only variant that reads the fitness values. It pushes the child out beyond the fitter parent, away from the weaker one:

    child = best + r × (best − worst)   where r is random in [0,1]

    Operator 3

    Mutation — and why the rate is tiny

    Flip-bit mutation on a binary chromosome: pick a gene, flip 0 ↔ 1.

    Before
    After

    On a permutation chromosome you cannot flip a bit without breaking the permutation, so you swap two positions instead — 1 5 3 2 6 → 1 2 3 5 6. On an 8-queens chromosome, mutation means moving one queen up or down inside its own column.

    CrossoverMutation
    Parents usedTwoOne
    RoleExploitation — recombines good building blocks that already existExploration — introduces genes that are nowhere in the population
    ProbabilityHigh, roughly 0.6 – 0.9Low, roughly 0.01
    Remove it and…The population stops mixing; the GA decays into parallel hill climbingDiversity collapses; the GA converges prematurely on a local optimum

    The rate has to stay low for a concrete reason worth writing out: crossover spends its effort assembling good building blocks, and a high mutation rate would tear them apart faster than they form, degrading the search into a random walk.

    Context

    Where GA sits among the local search algorithms

    Lecture 4 builds up to GA through three simpler algorithms. A “compare these” question is very answerable if you know what each one keeps in memory.

    AlgorithmStates heldHow it movesWeakness
    Hill climbing1Always to the best uphill neighbourLocal maxima, ridges, plateaux
    Simulated annealing1Uphill always; downhill with probability ΔE/TNeeds a cooling schedule
    Local beam searchkKeeps the k best successors of all k statesThe k states can crowd into one region
    Stochastic beamkPicks k successors at random, biased by valueSlower to converge
    Genetic algorithmkCombines two parents to make each successorMany parameters to tune

    Two supporting facts from Lecture 4 that often appear as short-answer marks:

    Local search is used when only the final state matters, not the path — 8-queens, VLSI layout, job-shop scheduling, vehicle routing, portfolio management. Its two advantages are constant memory and the ability to work in very large or continuous state spaces.

    Set D — written answers

    D1List the six steps of the basic genetic algorithm.

    Start — generate a random population of n chromosomes. Fitness — evaluate f(x) for each. New population — repeat selection, crossover, mutation and accepting until the new population is full. Replace — the new population becomes the current one. Test — if the termination condition is met, return the best solution. Loop — otherwise go back to the fitness step.

    Termination conditions: a solution is found, the generation limit is reached, fitness stops improving, or the time budget runs out.

    D2Give two differences between crossover and mutation, and say what happens if you remove each.

    Crossover takes two parents, mutation takes one. Crossover exploits genetic material already present by recombining it, while mutation explores by introducing genes that exist nowhere in the population. Crossover runs at a high probability (0.6–0.9), mutation at a low one (about 0.01).

    Remove crossover and the population stops mixing, so the GA decays into parallel hill climbing. Remove mutation and diversity collapses, so the GA converges prematurely on a local optimum and can never recover a gene the population has lost.

    D3What is elitism, and what failure does it prevent?

    Elitism copies the best one or two chromosomes unchanged into the next generation, bypassing crossover and mutation entirely.

    It prevents the best solution found so far from being destroyed. Without it, the fittest chromosome can be cut apart by crossover or damaged by a mutation, and the population's best fitness can actually decrease from one generation to the next.

    D4In this course's GA example, what happens to a chromosome whose f(x) is negative, and what does it mean for its chance of being selected?

    Its fitness is clamped to 0. String 5 in the worked example is 00101 = 5, giving f(5) = 12.5 − 15 = −2.5, which is recorded as 0.

    A fitness of 0 gives a probability of 0, so the chromosome receives no bin on the roulette wheel at all and can never be selected as a parent. It survives in the population but contributes nothing to the next generation.

    Before you hand it in

    Six ways to lose marks you've already earned

    • Reporting attacks when the question asked for fitness. Fitness is non-attacking pairs. For 24748552 the answer is 24, not 4.
    • Double-counting pairs. Always scan left to right, looking only rightward. Every pair then gets counted once.
    • Missing long diagonals. Six queens on one diagonal is not six attacks, it is C(6,2) = 15. Question A1 is built entirely on this.
    • Ignoring the stated index origin. Lecture 4's exercises number columns from 0; the 8Queen board diagram numbers rows from 1. The fitness value is the same either way, but your written positions must match the convention in the question.
    • Forgetting to clamp a negative f(x) to zero — and then giving that chromosome a bin on the wheel, which shifts every bin after it.
    • Writing that 5 bits encode 0 – 32. Five bits give 32 values, which is 0 – 31.

    One last check

    In any selection table, your probability column must sum to 1.00 and your last bin must end at 1.00. If either is off, you have an arithmetic slip somewhere above — and it takes five seconds to catch.
